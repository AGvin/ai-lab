# Documentation Requirements

## Requirements

- Use the reader-facing title `Jailbreaking` and introduce `LLM jailbreak` / `model jailbreak` as common contextual terms where useful.
- Define jailbreaking as an adversarial objective/attack family aimed at circumventing, disabling, removing, or otherwise defeating model or model-facing safety, alignment, refusal, moderation, or behavioral restrictions so the model produces behavior or output those restrictions were intended to withhold.
- Keep the concept objective-oriented rather than binding it to one attack mechanism. The defining question is whether intended behavioral restrictions are deliberately bypassed or removed; the delivery or modification path is classified separately.
- Preserve current taxonomy variation explicitly. NIST AI 100-2 E2025 defines a jailbreak narrowly as a direct prompting attack intended to circumvent restrictions on model outputs, while MITRE ATLAS 2026.07 `LLM Jailbreak` (`AML.T0054`) additionally includes direct modification of model weights or safety mechanisms. Do not present those source taxonomies as identical.
- Use `prompt-based jailbreaking` for the narrow interaction/input path consistent with NIST and common OWASP usage. Use a clearly qualified `model-modification` or equivalent variant when discussing the broader MITRE boundary so readers can tell that no inference-time prompt injection is required.
- Distinguish jailbreaking from `prompt-injection/`. Prompt injection is primarily an instruction/control-boundary attack mechanism that can pursue policy bypass, data disclosure, tool manipulation, workflow redirection, or other goals; jailbreaking is primarily the objective of defeating behavioral/safety restrictions. A prompt-based jailbreak can therefore be both without making the terms synonyms.
- Distinguish jailbreaking from `prompt-injection/indirect-prompt-injection/`. External malicious content can manipulate behavior through an indirect injection path; label the event a jailbreak as well only when the relevant safety/alignment/refusal restriction is actually the attack objective or defeated boundary. Do not imply that current NIST's direct-prompting jailbreak definition includes every indirect injection.
- Distinguish jailbreaking from ordinary unsafe or undesirable model output. A model can fail a safety policy, hallucinate harmful content, overcomply, or behave inconsistently without an adversarial attempt to bypass the intended restriction; that failure is not automatically a jailbreak.
- Distinguish jailbreaking from ordinary policy disagreement. A user asking for behavior outside a policy or an operator intentionally changing an authorized policy/configuration is not by itself a jailbreak attack; adversarial circumvention/removal of the intended restriction is the security boundary.
- Distinguish jailbreaking from authorized red teaming and safety evaluation. Evaluators may deliberately attempt jailbreaks to measure robustness; the attack technique remains the object of testing, while the authorized evaluation activity and concrete results belong to evaluation/evidence owners rather than being treated as a production compromise.
- Distinguish jailbreaking from content moderation as a broader governance/application control. External moderation systems can block or transform content independently of model alignment/refusal behavior; evading a generic moderation workflow is not automatically an LLM jailbreak unless the attacked boundary is the model/model-facing behavioral restriction in scope.
- Distinguish jailbreaking from direct model/artifact or software supply-chain compromise while allowing multi-label incidents. Unauthorized modification of weights, adapters, safety components, or model artifacts can be both a broader jailbreak under MITRE's objective boundary and an artifact/supply-chain compromise; do not collapse the compromise mechanism into the jailbreak concept.
- Treat a concrete `jailbroken`, `uncensored`, or safety-modified model/version as a concrete model/artifact fact rather than reusable concept identity. The concept owner explains the attack/state semantics; catalog/evidence owners retain exact model variants, releases, modifications, and current susceptibility.
- Explain attacker access as a material variable. Prompt-based attacks can require only query/conversation access, while model-modification variants require substantially stronger access to model artifacts, adaptation/update mechanisms, safety components, or deployment controls.
- Explain black-box and white-box conditions without assuming either. Query-only attackers can iterate against observed model behavior; attackers with model internals or artifact access can use different optimization or modification paths. Security claims must state the assumed access level.
- Explain that prompt-based jailbreak strategies can include adversarial instruction framing, role/persona or hypothetical framing, obfuscation/transformation, multi-turn escalation, constrained-format pressure, multilingual or multimodal representations, and automated/optimized prompt generation. Keep these as high-level attack families rather than publishing reusable bypass strings or model-specific recipes.
- Explain that model-modification jailbreak paths can include unauthorized adaptation, editing, removal/weakening of refusal or safety mechanisms, or equivalent changes to the model/safety stack. Keep implementation recipes, exact parameter changes, and concrete bypass artifacts outside the reusable concept owner.
- Treat attack success as restriction-specific. A jailbreak that defeats one refusal category, language, modality, tool-use policy, or interaction pattern does not prove all safeguards are bypassed or that the model is globally `uncensored`.
- Distinguish behavioral jailbreak success from downstream capability/impact. A model may produce previously refused text yet lack the capability, information, permissions, tools, or external access needed for the attacker's wider goal.
- Treat external authorization as independent from model behavior. Even a fully jailbroken model must not gain permission to read protected data, expose secrets, invoke privileged tools, modify records, execute code, spend assets, or take other consequential actions solely because its behavioral refusal was bypassed.
- Explain that system prompts, instruction hierarchy, model alignment, refusal training, classifiers, filters, and other model-facing guardrails are probabilistic/behavioral controls rather than substitutes for deterministic authentication, authorization, validation, least privilege, sandboxing, transaction rules, or approval gates.
- Explain defense in depth for prompt-based jailbreaking. Relevant layers can include model alignment/adversarial training, robust instruction/control separation, input/output policy checks, contextual validation, anomaly detection, rate/cost controls, human escalation, and independent downstream authorization. No single prompt, classifier, or filter should be presented as universally effective.
- Explain defense in depth for model-modification variants. Protect model/update/adaptation access, verify artifact provenance/integrity, restrict who can alter safety components, separate development and production authority, monitor unexpected model/configuration changes, and retain known-good reproducible artifacts for recovery.
- Explain the utility/security trade-off. Stronger refusal or filtering can increase false positives and block legitimate safety, research, medical, cybersecurity, educational, or other allowed work; robustness evaluation should measure both successful restriction enforcement and unnecessary refusal/utility regressions under the actual policy.
- Explain adaptive attack pressure. Public jailbreak families, automated search, transfer across models, new modalities, longer contexts, model/provider updates, and defender changes can invalidate prior robustness evidence; a one-time prompt set or past red-team pass is not durable proof of resistance.
- Require representative jailbreak evaluation to state the model/system/version, system prompt/policy/configuration, modality/language, conversation length, tool/retrieval context, attacker access/knowledge, targeted restriction, attack family, attempt budget, success criterion, and date where those factors materially affect interpretation.
- Separate attack success from harmfulness/capability scoring. `The model stopped refusing` and `the model produced an operationally useful harmful result` are different measurements and should not be conflated.
- Evaluate benign regression alongside attack resistance. Include legitimate requests near policy boundaries, multilingual/encoded but benign inputs, complex instructions, and allowed research/safety contexts as appropriate so a defense is not scored only by how often it refuses.
- Include multi-turn and stateful testing when the deployed interaction supports it. Safety behavior can change across accumulated context, previous refusals, memory, tool outputs, retrieval, or conversation state; single-turn evaluation alone can miss state-dependent bypasses.
- Include multimodal testing when the model/application accepts images, audio, video, files, or other modalities. Do not extrapolate text-only jailbreak robustness to different encoders, preprocessing paths, or modality combinations without evidence.
- Evaluate prompt-injection overlap explicitly when relevant. A prompt-based jailbreak may also violate instruction hierarchy; an indirect injection may additionally cause safety bypass. Record both the delivery mechanism and the defeated objective instead of forcing one label to erase the other.
- Evaluate deterministic downstream controls after behavioral bypass. A security test should verify that tool schemas, authorization, data boundaries, approval gates, sandboxing, transaction validation, and other external controls still hold even when the model is induced to ignore its own behavioral policy.
- Treat refusal consistency, safety-classifier results, and aggregate attack-success rates as evidence with scope, not guarantees. Results depend on attack distribution, evaluator definition, model stochasticity, sampling configuration, context, policy version, and test budget.
- Do not treat absence of success against famous or public jailbreak prompts as evidence of general robustness. Representative evaluation should include adaptive, transformed, multi-turn, automated, and deployment-specific attacks where the threat model justifies them.
- Explain detection separately from prevention. Suspicious prompt patterns, repeated policy-boundary probing, abrupt refusal changes, unusual output categories, safety-control configuration changes, model/artifact drift, or downstream action anomalies can support detection/investigation but do not prove a jailbreak has or has not occurred.
- Explain prompt-based incident response in terms of blast radius and consequences. Preserve relevant traces/evidence, stop or constrain affected sessions/automation, inspect downstream actions/data exposure, revoke or rotate exposed credentials where necessary, adjust controls, and re-test before restoring normal autonomy.
- Explain model-modification incident recovery separately. Identify the modified artifact/safety boundary, remove attacker access, restore a verified known-good model/configuration, invalidate compromised derivatives/caches where applicable, re-run integrity and behavioral evaluation, and monitor for reintroduction.
- Do not assume rollback alone is sufficient. If credentials, update authority, model registry access, adaptation pipelines, or deployment controls remain compromised, a restored model can be jailbroken again or replaced with another modified artifact.
- Explain that a safety bypass can create security, privacy, integrity, or safety impact depending on the surrounding system, but the jailbreak concept itself does not own every downstream consequence. Keep those impact semantics with the applicable trustworthy-AI/security/privacy/safety owners.
- Keep concrete jailbreak prompts, suffixes, encodings, optimized token sequences, exact exploit payloads, model-specific bypass recipes, current vulnerability claims, jailbreak benchmarks/results, red-team transcripts, detector thresholds, provider policy details, and project response playbooks with their applicable evidence/catalog/project owners.
- Use the canonical entity references as research inputs for the narrow NIST direct-prompting definition, the broader MITRE ATLAS objective/mechanism boundary, and OWASP application-security overlap with prompt injection when reader-facing rendering is activated.

## Validation

- Jailbreaking is defined by adversarial bypass/removal of intended model/model-facing behavioral restrictions rather than by any single prompt pattern or tool.
- Current NIST, MITRE ATLAS, and OWASP taxonomy differences remain explicit rather than being flattened into one supposedly universal hierarchy.
- Prompt injection and jailbreaking remain distinguishable: not every prompt injection is a jailbreak, and under the broader MITRE boundary not every jailbreak is a prompt injection.
- Unsafe output, ordinary policy disagreement, authorized policy changes, and authorized red-team activity are not automatically mislabeled as production jailbreak incidents.
- Prompt-based and model-modification jailbreak paths remain distinguishable by attacker access, mechanism, controls, and recovery needs.
- Model refusal/guardrail behavior is not treated as deterministic authorization for data, tools, secrets, transactions, or other consequential side effects.
- Evaluation measures both restriction-bypass success and benign utility/regression with explicit model/system/policy/attack scope.
- Defenses are presented as layered and adaptive risk reduction rather than universal jailbreak prevention guarantees.
- Concrete bypass payloads, current model susceptibility, jailbroken model artifacts, benchmark/red-team results, and project incident procedures remain outside the reusable concept owner.
