# Documentation Requirements

## Requirements

- Use the reader-facing title `Provenance`.
- Define provenance as information about the origin, derivation, attribution, processing, transformation, version/history, and involved agents/systems/activities that produced, modified, influenced, or delivered an entity, artifact, datum, model output, or other result.
- Keep the generic concept broader than one provenance standard, serialization, artifact type, or industry profile. W3C PROV, C2PA, software supply-chain attestations, dataset lineage, experiment tracking, and run traces are concrete provenance models/implementations, not the universal definition.
- Distinguish provenance from identity. A stable identifier tells which entity/version is being referenced; provenance additionally records relevant relationships explaining where it came from and how it reached the current state.
- Distinguish provenance from authenticity. Authenticity mechanisms can establish that an artifact or provenance record is associated with a signer/source and has not been altered under stated cryptographic assumptions, while provenance can exist without cryptographic authentication and authenticated provenance can still contain false or misleading assertions.
- Do not treat provenance as proof of factual truth, correctness, quality, authority, legality, originality, ownership, authorship, consent, licensing, or suitability. Provenance supplies evidence for those assessments but does not decide them automatically.
- Distinguish provenance from `grounding/`. Grounding asks whether evidence supports a particular generated/derived claim; provenance explains the origin/history/derivation of the evidence, intermediate artifact, or output. Provenance can help verify grounding without implying claim support by itself.
- Distinguish provenance from `citations/`. A citation is an explicit reference/attribution to a source or evidence location; provenance can describe how that source, citation target, derived artifact, or final output was created and transformed.
- Distinguish provenance from reproducibility. Provenance can record inputs, versions, transformations, agents, and activities, while reproducing a result additionally depends on executable procedure, environment, hidden state, nondeterminism, dependencies, hardware/runtime behavior, and other conditions.
- Distinguish provenance from audit logs. Logs are chronological operational records that can contribute provenance evidence; provenance is the semantic origin/derivation relationship reconstructed or recorded across relevant entities/activities/agents and need not equal the raw log stream.
- Distinguish provenance from chain of custody. Custody emphasizes possession/control and transfers between responsible parties; provenance can additionally include transformations, source derivation, generated intermediates, software/model versions, and automated processing even when custody never changes.
- Explain entity/activity/agent-style provenance generically: provenance commonly relates things/artifacts/data (`entities`) to operations/processes (`activities`) and responsible people/organizations/software/systems (`agents`), but equivalent models may use different terminology.
- Treat provenance granularity as use-dependent. A useful record may capture dataset release, source document, chunk, model checkpoint, adapter, prompt/configuration, tool call/result, generated image, transformation step, or final response depending on the verification/risk requirement; recording every internal event is not universally necessary or desirable.
- Preserve version and temporal boundaries where mutable inputs are involved. A filename, URL, model family, database record, API endpoint, prompt template, dataset name, policy, or software package can retain a label while its effective content/version changes.
- Explain derivation as a graph rather than assuming one linear history. Artifacts can have multiple inputs, branches, merges, transformations, reused components, copied/derived variants, and independently produced intermediates.
- Preserve transformation semantics where they materially affect interpretation. Parsing, OCR, chunking, filtering, summarization, translation, normalization, format conversion, quantization, fine-tuning, graph extraction, retrieval, or model generation can create a derived artifact whose provenance should retain its source dependency when verification requires it.
- Distinguish source/content provenance from process/run provenance. Content provenance records where an artifact/data item originated and how it changed; process provenance records which execution/configuration/tools/models/environments produced a particular result. Some applications need both and they must not be conflated.
- Treat generated AI intermediates as derived artifacts. Model-created summaries, labels, synthetic examples, graph edges, citations, code, tool plans, or rewritten content should preserve their generated status and upstream sources rather than silently becoming equivalent to source assertions.
- Explain provenance completeness as non-binary. Missing steps, detached metadata, screenshots/re-exports, format conversion, copying through non-aware tools, undocumented manual edits, or unavailable external systems can create partial provenance; absence of one record does not prove the artifact has no history.
- Do not infer trust from provenance presence alone. A malicious or low-authority actor can create internally consistent provenance, and a trustworthy artifact can lack machine-readable provenance; trust assessment uses provenance together with signer/source identity, authority, context, content evidence, threat model, and other signals.
- Explain tamper evidence carefully. Hashes, signatures, manifests, transparency logs, or content bindings can make unauthorized alteration detectable under their assumptions, but they do not guarantee that the original assertions were truthful or that all subsequent transformations were recorded.
- Explain provenance validation separately from provenance interpretation. A provenance record can be structurally/cryptographically valid while still containing ambiguous identities, missing lineage, unsupported assertions, stale metadata, misleading semantic labels, or sources unsuitable for the task.
- Preserve provenance of provenance when required. A provenance record itself can be generated, transformed, signed, merged, corrected, or imported; high-assurance systems may need to identify who/what produced the provenance assertions and under which policy.
- Explain identity resolution as a prerequisite for reliable lineage. Aliases, duplicated records, renamed resources, forks, mirrors, model aliases, moving URLs, and copied files can cause unrelated entities to be merged or one entity's history to be split unless identifiers/version rules are explicit.
- Explain external-reference durability. Provenance links to documents, repositories, APIs, object stores, model registries, or datasets can break or drift; stable IDs, hashes, versions, snapshots, archived locations, or repository-relative references can improve long-term resolution when appropriate.
- Treat sensitive provenance as data with its own privacy/security boundary. Provenance can reveal user identities, internal systems, file paths, prompts, source documents, workflow structure, geolocation, timestamps, device IDs, secrets, or business relationships; traceability does not override access-control/minimization requirements.
- Explain disclosure levels. A system can retain richer internal provenance than it exposes publicly; user-visible provenance/citations should reveal enough for the intended verification while respecting permissions, privacy, confidentiality, and intellectual-property constraints.
- Explain provenance for models and datasets without duplicating concrete catalog facts. Relevant relationships can include base model/checkpoint, adapters, training/fine-tuning data lineage, tokenizer/processor, transformation/conversion history, dataset sources/releases, generated/synthetic components, and evaluation artifacts, while exact concrete records stay with catalog/evidence/project owners.
- Explain provenance for generated media/content as a specialization. C2PA-style manifests and Content Credentials can bind signed assertions/history to assets and support tamper-evident content provenance, but their presence does not establish that depicted events or textual claims are factually true.
- Explain provenance for RAG/tool-based systems. Source-document/chunk lineage, index/graph transformations, retrieval/tool results, model/version, and final output can be linked so downstream evidence can be traced to upstream sources without claiming that every retrieved item actually supported every output claim.
- Keep concrete provenance manifests/records, W3C PROV documents, C2PA manifests, signatures/certificates, hashes, transparency-log entries, dataset/model cards, run traces, source inventories, licensing records, retention policies, and project-specific lineage schemas with their applicable catalog/specification/evidence/project/governance owners.
- Use the canonical entity references as research inputs for general interoperable provenance and modern verifiable content-provenance boundaries when reader-facing rendering is activated.

## Validation

- Provenance is not equated with truth, correctness, authenticity, authorship, ownership, licensing, or trustworthiness.
- Content/source provenance, process/run provenance, citations, grounding, audit logs, chain of custody, and reproducibility remain distinct concepts.
- Cryptographic validation/tamper evidence is not treated as proof that provenance assertions or content are factually true.
- Provenance can be partial, branching, multi-source, transformed, and version-sensitive rather than assumed to be a complete linear history.
- Generated AI intermediates retain derived/generated status and upstream lineage where required.
- Privacy/access constraints apply to provenance metadata as to other sensitive data.
- Concrete manifests, signatures, hashes, run traces, lineage schemas, and project policies remain outside the reusable provenance concept.
