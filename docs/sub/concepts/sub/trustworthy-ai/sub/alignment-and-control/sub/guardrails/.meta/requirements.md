# Documentation Requirements

## Requirements

- Use the reader-facing title `AI Guardrails` and introduce `guardrails` as the shorter term.
- Define guardrails as an AI-specific family of controls that observe, validate, constrain, transform, redirect, block, escalate, or monitor AI-system inputs, context, outputs, actions, model behavior, or relevant system state against stated policies, requirements, limits, or risk bounds.
- Treat guardrails as control mechanisms rather than the goals they serve. Safety, security, privacy, information integrity, reliability, compliance, cost control, and other objectives define desired properties/outcomes; a guardrail is one mechanism used to reduce the chance or impact of violating those objectives.
- Keep `alignment-and-control/` as the primary semantic owner because guardrails are mechanisms for constraining/validating/monitoring/intervening in AI behavior when model behavior alone is insufficient or uncertain. Express security, safety, privacy, governance, and other applicability through relations/navigation rather than duplicating canonical guardrail definitions.
- Distinguish guardrails from policy/requirements. A policy or requirement states what is allowed, prohibited, required, or escalated; a guardrail operationalizes or checks some portion of that policy at a concrete system boundary with defined inputs, authority, actions, and failure behavior.
- Distinguish guardrails from model alignment. Training, adaptation, preference optimization, instruction design, and other alignment methods shape model behavior; guardrails can operate before, around, after, or independently of the model and can intervene even when the model is unchanged.
- Do not imply that guardrails must be external to the model. Current usage includes data-, model-, application-, runtime-, and infrastructure-level controls; model safety classifiers, constrained decoding, validation models, or internal policy mechanisms can be guardrails when their control role is explicit.
- Do not imply that every system control should be called a guardrail. Authentication, authorization, cryptographic verification, filesystem/process isolation, transaction invariants, database constraints, and other conventional deterministic controls retain their precise security/engineering identities even when a system architecture informally groups them into a `guardrail` layer.
- Distinguish guardrails from content moderation. Moderation/classification of harmful, disallowed, illegal, or policy-sensitive content is one possible guardrail function; guardrails also cover schemas, tool/action mediation, resource limits, policy checks, approval/escalation, data handling, and other control objectives.
- For AI content-moderation controls, separate the **policy category**, **detection/classification evidence**, and **enforcement action**. The same detected category does not imply one universal action across products, users, jurisdictions, surfaces, or risk contexts, and a moderation model/classifier must not silently define the governing policy.
- Preserve context-sensitive moderation semantics. Quotation, reporting, education, research, support, satire, transformation, cultural/language context, target/audience, and other surrounding conditions can materially change a moderation decision even when similar terms or media are present; evaluate context handling rather than treating keyword/category presence as sufficient.
- Avoid one global moderation threshold or action rule when categories and consequences differ materially. Category-specific thresholds, confidence/uncertainty handling, consequence-aware actions, and routing of ambiguous or high-impact cases to stronger controls or human review can reduce both harmful false negatives and broad legitimate-content suppression.
- Keep moderation appeals/recourse, platform/community rules, legal obligations, statements of reasons, and human complaint processes with governance/legal/oversight owners. A technical moderation guardrail should expose enough decision context/provenance for review where required, but it does not own the policy legitimacy or appeal procedure itself.
- Distinguish guardrails from evaluation. Guardrail evaluation measures whether controls work under defined conditions; the guardrail itself is the deployed/predeployment control mechanism, while benchmark/red-team/evaluation results remain evidence owned elsewhere.
- Explain control placement explicitly. Useful placement categories can include data/ingestion, model, user/input, prompt/context/retrieval, output/post-processing, agent/workflow state, tool/action execution, infrastructure, and monitoring/audit layers; exact categories can overlap and should be tied to what the control can observe and influence.
- Explain that placement changes visibility and effectiveness. A network/API gateway may see individual requests but lack full conversation/tool state; an application/agent-layer guardrail can have richer context but also more privilege and complexity; a model-level control can shape generation but cannot independently enforce external authorization.
- Explain input guardrails as controls that inspect, validate, normalize, classify, reject, transform, or route user/external inputs before or while they enter the model/application. Examples should remain conceptual rather than product-specific.
- Explain context/retrieval guardrails as controls around external documents, memory, tool outputs, retrieved evidence, system context, or other model-visible data. They can validate source/format/policy properties but do not make untrusted content authoritative merely because it passed a filter.
- Explain output guardrails as controls that validate, classify, redact, transform, constrain, reject, regenerate, or escalate generated output before it reaches a user or downstream component. Passing an output guardrail is evidence under that control's tested scope, not proof of correctness or safety.
- Explain action/tool guardrails as controls that inspect or mediate proposed tool calls, arguments, resource scopes, plans, transactions, or side effects before execution. Consequential actions still require deterministic authorization and business-rule validation appropriate to the operation.
- Explain model-level guardrails as model-internal or model-adjacent mechanisms that constrain or classify behavior, such as safety/alignment behavior, constrained generation, specialized guard models, or policy critics. Keep detailed model/training definitions with their canonical owners.
- Explain monitoring/audit guardrails carefully. Detection, telemetry, anomaly monitoring, policy-violation alerts, or post-action checks can reduce time-to-detection/response even when they cannot prevent the original event; do not call detection equivalent to prevention.
- Explain infrastructure guardrails as surrounding controls that constrain compute, network, storage, process, identity, deployment, or resource behavior. Keep exact sandboxing, authorization, secret-management, and infrastructure-security semantics with their specific owners rather than redefining them here.
- Classify guardrails by enforcement style where useful: deterministic/rule-based, statistical/model-based, hybrid, or human-mediated. Avoid implying that one style is universally stronger; effectiveness depends on policy expressibility, context, adversary, cost, latency, and failure mode.
- Explain model-based guardrails as probabilistic components subject to their own model errors, distribution shift, adversarial inputs, and version drift. A guard model, LLM judge, classifier, or refusal model must not silently become a deterministic authority boundary.
- Explain deterministic guardrails as controls whose decision logic is reproducible for the same explicit state/rules, while still acknowledging specification/configuration bugs, incomplete policy coverage, stale data, compromised inputs, and implementation defects.
- Keep critical authorization independent from probabilistic guardrails. Secrets, privileged data, identity/role scopes, financial/irreversible transactions, infrastructure changes, code execution, record mutation, and comparable high-impact actions require deterministic checks at the operation boundary even if AI guardrails also screen intent/content.
- Explain human-mediated guardrails as escalation, approval, review, or override controls for conditions the automated system should not resolve autonomously. Detailed human-oversight interaction semantics remain with `human-ai-interaction/oversight-and-intervention/`.
- Require each guardrail to have a stated protected objective/policy, observation point, input/state available to it, decision/enforcement authority, intervention behavior, failure behavior, and downstream assumptions. `Add a guardrail` alone is not an implementable control specification.
- Explain fail-open versus fail-closed behavior. When a guardrail errors, times out, is unavailable, or cannot classify, the system must intentionally choose whether to continue, block, degrade, retry, route, or escalate according to consequence/risk; the safer default varies by control and use case.
- Explain that guardrails can transform or redirect rather than only allow/deny. Possible control actions include sanitization, redaction, constrained regeneration, safer fallback, reduced capability/tool scope, read-only mode, additional verification, human review, or refusal.
- Explain guardrail composition. Multiple controls can operate serially, in parallel, hierarchically, or at different boundaries; ordering can change what later controls observe and can create bypasses, duplicated blocking, conflicting decisions, or inconsistent policy semantics.
- Require policy precedence/conflict handling when multiple guardrails disagree. The system should define which control has authority, whether any `deny` dominates, when escalation is required, and how contradictory outputs are recorded rather than leaving resolution to incidental execution order.
- Explain statefulness. Some risks require conversation history, agent trajectory, accumulated resource use, prior tool calls, tenant/session identity, memory changes, or repeated attempts; a stateless per-message guardrail can miss patterns that a stateful control can detect.
- Explain multi-modal coverage explicitly. Text-only controls cannot be assumed to govern instructions or harmful content embedded in images, audio, video, files, structured payloads, code, or tool arguments unless those modalities pass through an applicable validated control path.
- Explain policy and representation boundaries. Encoding, translation, obfuscation, formatting, tokenization, parser differences, structured data, long contexts, and multimodal transformations can change what a control sees; normalization can help but can also destroy meaning or introduce new ambiguity.
- Treat untrusted data entering a guardrail as untrusted after it leaves unless the guardrail specifically establishes a validated property. A successful malware check, format validation, toxicity score, or prompt-injection screen does not implicitly establish authorization, factual correctness, provenance, or harmlessness for unrelated properties.
- Treat guardrail output/metadata as security-sensitive control state where applicable. Scores, allow/deny decisions, policy labels, override tokens, human approvals, or routing decisions can themselves become attack targets and may require integrity, access-control, provenance, and audit protections.
- Explain bypass/evasion as a first-class failure mode for adversarial guardrails. Attackers can adapt prompts, context, modalities, encodings, multi-turn behavior, source content, or tool sequences to exploit blind spots; a static test set is not durable proof of adversarial robustness.
- Preserve the current-source limitation that no finite fixed set of AI guardrails should be presented as universally robust against adaptive adversarial prompting. Frame this as a reason for continuous evaluation/update and defense in depth, not as evidence that guardrails are useless.
- Explain false negatives and false positives separately. False negatives allow unwanted behavior; false positives block or distort legitimate use. Guardrail quality must be evaluated against both risk reduction and user/task utility rather than maximizing block/refusal rate.
- Explain abstention/uncertainty where supported. A guardrail can route ambiguous cases to a stronger control, human review, safer mode, or refusal instead of forcing an unreliable binary decision.
- Explain latency, cost, and capacity effects. Additional model calls, classifiers, validators, human review, or complex policy engines can materially change response time, throughput, availability, and cost; these trade-offs are part of control design and evaluation.
- Explain cascading/feedback effects. Regeneration loops, repeated guard-model calls, fallback chains, or interacting controls can create latency amplification, runaway cost, infinite loops, or repeated unsafe attempts unless bounded explicitly.
- Explain observability as part of control operation. Record the guardrail/policy/version, relevant decision, reason/category, timing, intervention, override/escalation, and correlation to downstream action where appropriate, while respecting privacy/security requirements for logged content.
- Keep logging distinct from proof. Logs can support audit and incident response, but missing events, redaction, compromised telemetry, or incorrect classification can limit their completeness and accuracy.
- Require configuration/version boundaries. Policy rules, thresholds, classifiers, prompts, guard models, allowlists, schemas, model/provider versions, and application placement can change control behavior; guardrail evidence should identify materially relevant versions.
- Require representative guardrail evaluation to include normal allowed cases, disallowed/risky cases, ambiguous/boundary cases, multilingual/multimodal cases where applicable, distribution shift, failure/unavailability behavior, and adversarial/evasion cases when the threat model includes attackers.
- Match metrics to control purpose. Relevant measures can include precision/recall/false-positive/false-negative rates, policy-violation escape rate, attack success rate, intervention correctness, benign task success/utility, latency, cost, availability, escalation load, and downstream harm/side-effect prevention when measurable.
- Separate component performance from system outcome. A high classifier F1 or low jailbreak rate does not prove the overall application is secure/safe if tools, permissions, retrieval, routing, fallback, or downstream validation remain weak.
- Test guardrail failure paths deliberately. Timeouts, dependency outages, malformed responses, contradictory control results, stale configuration, capacity exhaustion, partial deployment, or unavailable human reviewers can produce more dangerous behavior than ordinary positive/negative classifications.
- Explain continuous monitoring/update as appropriate for mutable/adversarial contexts. New attack patterns, changed policies, model/provider updates, new tools/modalities, data shift, and observed production failures can require guardrail revalidation or revision.
- Treat guardrail changes as potentially behavior-changing deployments. Tightening can increase legitimate refusals or operational blocks; loosening can increase exposure; changing ordering/placement/model thresholds can alter end-to-end behavior even when the base model is unchanged.
- Explain resilience when a guardrail is bypassed. Reduce blast radius through independent permissions, sandboxing, transaction limits, staged execution, approvals, monitoring, rollback/recovery, and graceful degradation rather than assuming the outermost AI filter will always stop the event.
- Keep concrete guardrail products/frameworks/models, provider-specific features, exact moderation/policy text, rules/allowlists, classifier prompts, category taxonomies, thresholds, benchmark/red-team results, current vulnerability claims, operational dashboards, and project-specific control stacks with their applicable catalog/evidence/project/governance owners.
- Use the canonical entity references as research inputs for broad lifecycle guardrail scope, multi-layer/runtime placement, probabilistic-versus-deterministic boundaries, adaptive-robustness limitations, and AI content-filter/human-moderation separation when reader-facing rendering is activated.

## Validation

- Guardrails are presented as AI control mechanisms, not as a synonym for safety, security, alignment, moderation, policy, evaluation, or authorization.
- The primary alignment/control owner does not absorb the safety/security/privacy/governance objectives that guardrails may support.
- Data/model/application/runtime/infrastructure placements and their visibility/authority differences remain explicit.
- AI content moderation is represented as one guardrail/control specialization with policy, classifier/evidence, enforcement action, context, uncertainty, and human/governance boundaries kept distinct rather than materialized as a redundant canonical leaf.
- Model-based/probabilistic guardrails are not treated as deterministic permission or safety-critical enforcement boundaries.
- Passing one guardrail establishes only the property that control actually validates under its tested assumptions, not generic trustworthiness.
- False positives, false negatives, adaptive bypass, state/context blind spots, latency/cost, composition, and fail-open/fail-closed behavior remain first-class control concerns.
- Evaluation covers benign utility and failure/adversarial conditions rather than only blocked unsafe examples.
- No static finite guardrail set is presented as universal immunity to adaptive adversarial prompting.
- Concrete products, policies, configurations, moderation taxonomies/thresholds, evaluation results, and project enforcement stacks remain outside the reusable guardrails concept owner.
