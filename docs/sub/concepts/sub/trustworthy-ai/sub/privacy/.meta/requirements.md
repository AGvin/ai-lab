# Documentation Requirements

## Requirements

- Use the reader-facing title `AI Privacy` and introduce `data privacy` as the common legacy/general term.
- Define AI privacy as managing risks and requirements arising from the collection, processing, inference, transformation, storage, transmission, retention, sharing, reuse, and deletion of personal, confidential, sensitive, or otherwise privacy-relevant information across an AI system's lifecycle and data paths.
- Distinguish privacy from security and confidentiality. Security controls can protect data against unauthorized access while a fully secure system can still create privacy risk through excessive collection, incompatible reuse, inference, retention, profiling, or disclosure; confidentiality is one possible privacy/security property, not the whole privacy concept.
- Distinguish privacy from anonymity/pseudonymization. Removing direct identifiers or replacing them with tokens can reduce exposure but does not prove anonymity when remaining attributes, linked datasets, embeddings, model outputs, or external information permit re-identification or singling out.
- Treat data residency as a location property/constraint with an explicitly stated scope rather than a separate AI-native concept leaf. Current authoritative terminology is not perfectly uniform: for example, Government of Canada digital guidance defines residency as the physical/geographic location of data while at rest, while many operational discussions also ask where processing occurs. State separately where data is **stored**, **processed**, and **accessed** instead of hiding those differences under one label.
- Distinguish data residency from data localization. Residency describes or constrains where scoped data/processing is located; localization is commonly a legal/administrative requirement that mandates or restricts processing/storage to a specified jurisdiction. A workload can have a chosen residency without being legally localization-mandated, and cross-border-transfer conditions are not automatically localization requirements.
- Distinguish data residency/localization from data sovereignty. Sovereignty concerns jurisdictional/legal authority or control over data and can depend on provider/legal relationships beyond the physical server location; a data copy residing in one country does not by itself prove that no other jurisdiction, entity, or lawful-access regime can affect it.
- Distinguish residency from privacy/compliance outcomes. A regionally confined deployment can still collect too much data, retain it too long, expose it to too many actors, train/reuse it incompatibly, or violate another obligation; conversely, cross-border processing is not automatically unlawful or privacy-violating without the applicable legal/policy context.
- Apply privacy analysis across the complete AI data flow rather than only the model API call: user inputs/prompts, uploaded files, source documents, retrieval corpora/chunks, embeddings/vector stores, caches, model/provider logs, traces/observability, tool calls/results, agent/application memory, training/fine-tuning/evaluation data, generated outputs, backups, exports, and human-review surfaces can each retain or expose information.
- Apply residency/location analysis across that same end-to-end flow. Record relevant storage, processing, access, replication, backup/disaster-recovery, telemetry/monitoring, support/administration, subprocessor, control-plane, failover, and derived-data locations where the requirement or risk model makes them material; checking only the primary model endpoint or company headquarters is insufficient.
- Treat remote/support access and cross-border operational access as separate facts from storage residency. Applicable privacy/transfer regimes can care about who receives or accesses data and from where even when the primary data store remains in the selected region; do not infer legal transfer status from geography alone without jurisdiction-specific analysis.
- Treat regional failover, replication, backup, and disaster recovery as explicit residency design choices. A service can satisfy normal-operation locality while failing over, restoring, or replicating outside that boundary unless the concrete service/contract/configuration constrains those paths.
- Explain residency trade-offs without treating them as automatic privacy/security gains. Region restrictions can reduce provider/model availability, failover options, resilience, latency performance, or operational flexibility and can concentrate data/infrastructure; assess the actual privacy, security, resilience, legal, and business effects rather than assuming locality is uniformly safer.
- Prefer data minimization and purpose-aware processing where compatible with the task: collect/send/retain only the fields, granularity, time span, and derived representations necessary for the explicitly permitted purpose rather than forwarding complete records by default.
- Define access/authorization boundaries separately from model behavior. Embedding or retrieving a confidential document, placing sensitive context into a prompt, or storing it in memory/cache does not preserve the source access policy automatically; downstream stores and retrieval paths require policy-aware isolation/enforcement.
- Treat derived representations as potentially sensitive. Embeddings, summaries, classifications, model features, synthetic records, metadata, and aggregate statistics can retain or reveal information about source individuals/data depending on construction and attack/context; do not label them `non-sensitive` merely because raw text/images are absent.
- Explain inference/privacy leakage risks. AI systems can infer attributes not explicitly provided, memorize/reproduce training or context data, expose retrieved private information, or correlate records across interactions; privacy review therefore includes outputs and derived behavior as well as stored inputs.
- Define retention and deletion semantics across copies/derived stores. Deleting a source record does not necessarily remove derived embeddings, caches, logs, backups, fine-tuning datasets/checkpoints, evaluation artifacts, exports, or third-party/provider copies; concrete systems need documented propagation/limitations rather than promising universal deletion guarantees.
- Explain provider/external-service processing boundaries. When data leaves a controlled environment, model/API/tool providers can have separate retention, training/use, logging, subprocessors, region, encryption, access, support, replication, failover, or deletion behavior; mutable provider terms and regional capabilities are concrete evidence/catalog facts and must be checked for the actual service/version/contract.
- Treat provider `regional`, `EU`, `sovereign`, `local`, `no training`, or similar labels as scoped claims that require exact product/contract documentation. A product region may cover some data-plane operations but not every control-plane, support, telemetry, safety, abuse-monitoring, backup, or subprocessor path unless the provider explicitly commits to those boundaries.
- Distinguish local/self-hosted processing from automatic privacy. Local execution can reduce third-party disclosure and improve control, but privacy still depends on local access controls, logs, backups, malware/administration, shared devices, retention, network exposure, and the legitimacy/necessity of the processing itself.
- Explain privacy-enhancing techniques as mechanism families rather than guarantees: minimization, access isolation, encryption, tokenization/pseudonymization, aggregation, redaction, de-identification, differential privacy, secure computation, local processing, retention limits, and other PETs address different risks and can have utility/cost/accuracy trade-offs.
- Make clear that redaction/de-identification is context-dependent. Free text, images/audio, combinations of quasi-identifiers, metadata, and model-generated descriptions can leak identity or sensitive attributes even when obvious fields are removed.
- Treat multi-tenant caches, retrieval indexes, session/agent memory, and observability as explicit privacy boundaries. Cache hits, similarity search, trace viewers, shared memory, or debugging tools must not expose another user's/tenant's data or membership through improper isolation.
- Preserve consent, notice, purpose, access/correction/deletion rights, children's/special-category data, sectoral rules, cross-border processing, localization requirements, sovereignty questions, and other legal obligations as jurisdiction/use-specific governance requirements rather than universalizing one regulatory regime in the concept definition.
- Require privacy/residency evidence to identify data categories/subjects, sources, purpose, processors/recipients, exact location scope where relevant, stores/derived artifacts, replication/backup/failover paths, support/subprocessor access, access boundaries, retention/deletion behavior, and assumptions/known limitations. A generic `hosted in region X` or `we do not train on your data` statement is not a complete privacy or residency model.
- Keep concrete privacy policies/contracts, jurisdictions/regulations, localization/transfer obligations, provider retention/training terms, DPA/subprocessor lists, data inventories/classifications, retention periods, encryption settings, residency regions, service regional-capability matrices, deletion workflows, incident records, and project risk decisions with their applicable governance/catalog/project/evidence owners.
- Use the canonical entity references as research inputs for privacy-enhanced AI, re-identification/inference risks, data-processing privacy-risk boundaries, residency/localization/sovereignty distinctions, and third-party GAI location/compliance scope when reader-facing rendering is activated.

## Validation

- Privacy is not equated with security, encryption, confidentiality, local hosting, data residency, data localization, data sovereignty, anonymization, or `not used for training` alone.
- Data residency, localization, sovereignty, cross-border transfer, and privacy/compliance are distinguished rather than used interchangeably.
- Residency claims state whether they cover storage, processing, access, replication, backup/disaster recovery, support/subprocessors, and failover instead of assuming one regional endpoint covers the entire AI workflow.
- Privacy analysis covers prompts, retrieval/embeddings, caches/memory, logs/traces, tools, training/evaluation data, outputs, backups, and external processors as applicable.
- Pseudonymized/derived/embedded data is not automatically described as anonymous or non-sensitive.
- Access policies are not assumed to survive copying/indexing/context insertion without explicit downstream enforcement.
- Deletion/retention claims account for derived copies and known technical/provider limitations rather than implying instant universal erasure.
- Jurisdiction-specific legal obligations, localization/transfer rules, sovereignty decisions, and mutable provider regional terms remain outside the reusable privacy concept owner.
- Concrete policies, regions, retention values, processors, technical settings, and privacy/residency decisions remain with their applicable governance/project/catalog/evidence owners.
