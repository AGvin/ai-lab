# Documentation Requirements

## Requirements

- Use the reader-facing title `Oversight and Intervention` and introduce `human oversight` as the primary human-AI form represented by the merged legacy `human-in-the-loop` source.
- Define oversight as arrangements through which responsible people or human roles can monitor, review, question, approve, reject, redirect, pause, override, escalate, or otherwise intervene in AI-supported decisions or actions at defined points in the system lifecycle or runtime.
- Treat `human in the loop`, `human on the loop`, `human in command`, and related labels as varying taxonomies rather than universally standardized categories. Explain the actual human role, decision authority, timing, and intervention capacity instead of relying on the label alone.
- Distinguish constitutive human participation in producing a decision/output from corrective oversight over an otherwise executing process. Both can involve humans, but they create different causal/control relationships and should not be collapsed into one vague `HITL` claim.
- Explain that meaningful oversight requires more than human presence: the overseer needs appropriate authority, information/evidence, competence, time, interface support, and a feasible intervention path before consequences become irreversible.
- Distinguish monitoring, review, approval, intervention, and accountability. A person can monitor without approving, approve without continuously monitoring, or intervene without being the ultimate governance/accountability owner.
- Make clear that human reviewers are not inherently independent or infallible. Automation bias, alert fatigue, overreliance, insufficient context, weak incentives, time pressure, and model-framed evidence can reduce oversight effectiveness.
- Relate oversight to `agents-and-autonomy/autonomy-and-control/` without duplicating it: autonomy defines how independently the system may act; oversight/intervention defines human observation/control relationships. More autonomy can require different oversight, but neither dimension alone determines the other.
- Avoid a universal rule that every AI system requires a human approval gate. Oversight mechanisms should be justified by task, consequence, reversibility, regulation/policy, system capability, reliability, and organizational context.
- Preserve high-impact approval/escalation examples from the legacy source as procedural migration material for later learning/system-control owners; do not encode one fixed list of actions or one approval workflow as the abstract concept definition.
- Keep concrete approval UX, legal/regulatory obligations, organizational role matrices, timeout/default behavior, incident procedures, product modes, and domain-specific review checklists with their applicable governance, security, engineering, learning, catalog, or project owners.
- Use the canonical entity references as research inputs for human-role, oversight-capacity, and HITL/HOTL terminology boundaries when reader-facing rendering is activated.

## Validation

- The page does not create a canonical `human-in-the-loop` child from the legacy slug.
- Human presence or a nominal approval step is not presented as automatic evidence of effective oversight, safety, or accountability.
- HITL/HOTL/HIC terminology is not treated as one universally standardized taxonomy.
- Human oversight is distinguished from autonomy, authorization/permissions, governance accountability, and deterministic verification.
- The page acknowledges human and organizational failure modes rather than treating reviewers as perfect validators.
- Legacy approval/workflow procedures are preserved for their proper owners rather than copied as a universal oversight recipe.
